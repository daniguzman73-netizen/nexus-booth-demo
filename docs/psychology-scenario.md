## The AI-generated response (shown on Screen 5)

> Recent research presents a complex picture of how social media affects adolescent mental health. Several large-scale studies have found correlations between heavy social media use and increased rates of anxiety and depression in teenagers, particularly among adolescent girls [1]. However, the relationship is not straightforward — moderating factors such as the type of platform, content consumed, and individual vulnerability appear to play significant roles [2].
>
> A 2023 longitudinal analysis suggested that passive scrolling is more strongly linked to negative outcomes than active engagement [3], while other work has emphasized the protective role of online peer support for marginalized youth [4]. More recent neuroimaging studies have begun to identify potential neural correlates of habitual social media checking in adolescents [5], though these findings remain preliminary.
>
> Overall, the evidence suggests that *how* adolescents use social media matters more than *whether* they use it.

## Notes for SME content review

When replacing this placeholder content, the SME should verify each of the following:

1. The verified citation is genuinely real and accessible from typical academic libraries.
2. The predatory journal example is genuinely on a credible watchlist (and won't trigger legal concerns about defamation — pick one with broad consensus).
3. The preprint exists on PsyArXiv (or similar) and has not been published in a peer-reviewed venue at time of show.
4. The inaccessible paper is real, peer-reviewed, but genuinely hard to access for most academic libraries.
5. The fabricated citation does *not* accidentally match a real paper. Search Web of Science before finalizing.
6. The "alternatives" Nexus suggests are real, accessible, and topically appropriate.
7. Tone of the AI response matches typical ChatGPT/Claude output (slight hedging, "research suggests" language, mild over-confidence on the fabricated finding).
